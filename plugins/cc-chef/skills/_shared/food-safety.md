# Food safety

Use this file whenever a recipe, option or guideline involves leftovers, reheating, cooling, storage or a food that carries a known hazard: `create-recipe` loads it before it accepts cooked leftovers or high-risk ingredients as inputs, `rework-recipe` when a rework changes storage, make-ahead or reheating advice, and `dietary-guidelines` when a household member is an infant, toddler, pregnant, elderly or immunocompromised. Section 1 gives the decision rules, section 2 the authority table and the plugin default, sections 3-5 cooling, reheating and freezing, section 6 high-risk foods, section 7 vulnerable groups, section 8 wording and questions, section 9 boundaries, section 10 a checklist, section 11 evidence notes. It is guidance for everyday home cooking, not medical advice and not a substitute for the local authority's current page.

Conventions: `(to verify)` marks a claim that only one research report made. "Default" is what the plugin does when reports or authorities disagree. Evidence tags: official = published authority guidance (as reported); empirical = studies or surveillance data; practitioner = kitchen or textbook heuristic; design = a rule chosen for this plugin. Temperatures are in Celsius with Fahrenheit where an authority uses it. Limits change; the figures here come from authority pages as the reports saw them.

## 1. Decision rules

| #   | Rule                                                                                                                                                                             | Tag      |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------- |
| 1   | Use the user's registered region. If no region is known, use the strictest rule in section 2 and say that this is what is being done                                             | design   |
| 2   | Never average authorities into an invented international rule; show the rule of the authority being followed                                                                     | design   |
| 3   | Keep two judgements apart: is the food eligible at all (identity, history, label), and which eligible food should be used first (section 2 of `cooking-from-what-is-on-hand.md`) | design   |
| 4   | Ask only what decides eligibility: when it was cooked, how fast it was chilled, where it was kept, whether the pack was opened. Do not turn a safety check into an interrogation | design   |
| 5   | Unknown history of a high-risk food means leave it out of the plan; do not suggest that a hot pan or a long cook rescues it                                                      | design   |
| 6   | Reheating does not reset the clock: the original cooking and storage timeline travels with leftovers into any new dish                                                           | official |
| 7   | When the household includes a vulnerable person (section 7), apply the stricter end of every range, or the group-specific rule if one exists                                     | official |
| 8   | Appearance, smell and taste never certify safety, and no safety judgement is made from a photo (section 9)                                                                       | official |
| 9   | When in doubt about a high-risk food, advise discarding rather than rescuing                                                                                                     | design   |
| 10  | Distinguish a safety rule from a quality preference in the wording ("this is a safety limit" versus "it tastes better fresh")                                                    | design   |

Date labels: a "use by" date marks safety, while "best before" marks quality and is not a hard stop. Opened products need the opening date and the manufacturer's after-opening instruction; there is no sound international rule such as "opened dairy lasts seven days".

## 3. Cooling and storage

| Step                     | Rule                                                                                                                    | Notes                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Time at room temperature | Chill within 2 h (1 h in a hot room or above 32 °C); rice and starches within 1 h                                       | Do not leave a pot to cool on the stove overnight                                                       |
| Portioning               | Divide stews, stocks, curries and rice into shallow containers (about 5 cm or 2 inches deep at most)                    | Large volumes cool slowly in the middle, the main risk with Clostridium perfringens and Bacillus cereus |
| Hold hot food            | At 60 °C or above if kept hot at all                                                                                    | BfR and EFSA framing: illness mostly follows food kept between about 4 and 60 °C                        |
| Danger range             | B. cereus can grow from about 7 to 48 °C, and the emetic toxin forms best between about 20 and 40 °C (BfR, as reported) | The wider the time spent in this range, the more risk                                                   |
| Labelling                | Write the cooking date on the container                                                                                 | Store `cooked_at` and the storage place in the inventory                                                |
| Fridge use               | Keep at or below 4 °C; keep raw and cooked food apart (separation of raw and cooked is one of EFSA's five keys)         | official                                                                                                |
| Refrigerated limits      | Section 2                                                                                                               | The clock starts at cooking, not at opening the container                                               |
| Items under oil          | Do not keep cooked vegetables, garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature                                               | Botulism risk from anaerobic storage (single report, to verify)                                         |

## 4. Reheating

| Rule                                          | Detail                                                                                                                                    | Tag                  |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Reach the target                              | Section 2.2: steaming hot throughout; probe 75 °C at the core where a thermometer is used, 74 °C is the US figure                         | official             |
| Soups, sauces, gravies                        | Bring to a rolling boil                                                                                                                   | official (FSIS)      |
| Microwave                                     | Spread food flat, cover, stir halfway, let it stand, check in several spots; bubbling at the edges does not show an even core temperature | practitioner         |
| Reheat once, and only what will be eaten      | Do not cycle a whole batch in and out of the fridge                                                                                       | official             |

Spinach and nitrite, in more detail. The old claim "reheated spinach is poisonous" is imprecise; the mechanism is nitrate converted to nitrite by bacteria when cooked greens are kept warm or cool slowly. BfR's German material advises avoiding long warm holding, cooling quickly and refrigerating; its English FAQ says cooked spinach should not be reheated; German consumer advice generally accepts careful reheating of properly chilled leftovers by adults and not for infants. BfR also relays a caution for children with bacterial gastrointestinal infections, drawing on EFSA. Default: cool quickly and chill; reheat once only for adults; do not serve reheated leftovers of spinach, chard or beetroot to infants; in a recipe, cook spinach for the infant's portion fresh.

Items not covered in the reports with a figure: minced meat core temperature, egg storage times, raw-milk cheese outside pregnancy and infant rules, sprout handling beyond avoidance in pregnancy. Do not invent numbers; use the plugin default windows and flag the gap.

## 7. Vulnerable groups

"Vulnerable" here means infants, toddlers, pregnant people, older adults and immunocompromised people. For all of them, take the stricter end of every range in section 2, and apply the group rule below on top.

Country-specific contested details: egg rules in pregnancy, the age to avoid whole nuts (3, 4 or 5), the exact listeria food list, fish limits and added-sugar age cut-offs. Ask for the country and use that authority's list; never silently use one country's list for another.

Handling rules for the three consuming skills:

- Hard constraints are never relaxed: honey under 12 months, choking-safe preparation, pregnancy listeria and raw-food rules, declared allergies.
- A household recipe with a baby or toddler at the table sets their portion aside before adding salt, honey, whole nuts or high-risk ingredients.
- A conflict that cannot be resolved produces a safe alternative or no recipe, not a best guess.

## 8. Wording and questions

| Situation                              | Ask or say                                                                                                            |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Leftover rice offered for fried rice   | "When was the rice cooked, and did it go into the fridge within an hour or so?"                                       |
| Leftover cooked meat or fish           | "When was it cooked and how long was it out before the fridge?"                                                       |
| Chicken or fish of unknown state       | "Is it raw or cooked, and what is the use-by date?"                                                                   |
| Any leftover close to the limit        | Give the local limit and the date it expires; offer to freeze now                                                     |
| Region not known                       | "Which country are you in? I use that to pick the right storage rules. Without it I follow the strictest ones."       |
| Household member in a vulnerable group | "Who is eating, and is anyone pregnant, a baby, elderly or with a weakened immune system?"                            |
| Citing a rule                          | Name the authority and the figure ("UK FSA: cooked rice within 24 hours"); mark it as a safety rule, not a preference |
| Food of doubtful history               | "I cannot judge that from here. If you cannot place when it was cooked, I would not use it."                          |

Keep questions to what decides eligibility. A normal-looking item is not evidence of safety, and a safety answer never goes out as a verdict on a photo.

## 9. Boundaries

- No safety judgement from photos, appearance, texture or smell. Bacteria that cause food poisoning cannot be smelled, and spoilage organisms are only one part of the picture.
- Condition seen in a photo (limp leaves, a mouldy patch) may choose technique or trigger a prompt ("check the date on that"), never a verdict.
- Visible mould on soft food: discard. Firm foods such as hard cheese can sometimes be trimmed if the local guidance allows it. Sprouting potatoes: cut away sprouts and green or rotten parts; discard if badly affected.
- Do not tell a user a food is safe to eat. Say what the cited authority's limit is and whether the stated history fits within it.
- Allergen status and cross-contact are not judged from appearance either; for severe allergy, direct the user to the product label and the manufacturer.
- Individual medical situations (kidney disease, immune suppression, pregnancy complications, infant feeding problems) go to a clinician.

## 10. Checklist before accepting a recipe or option

1. Region known, or strictest default applied and stated?
2. Every cooked leftover has a cooking date and a storage history, or is left out?
3. Rice and starchy leftovers: within 24 h, cooled fast, never reheated before?
4. Raw poultry, minced meat, fish: within 2 days of purchase or frozen?
5. Large batches portioned shallow and chilled within 2 h?
6. Reheating step gives both the probe figure and the visual cue, once only?
7. Any nitrate-rich leftover vegetables for an infant? Replace with freshly cooked.
8. Any honey, whole nuts, raw egg, sprouts, unpasteurised cheese or high-mercury fish for an infant, toddler or pregnant person? Remove or adapt, and say so.
9. Make-ahead or storage advice at the end of the recipe stays within the leftover window and gives a freezer option?
10. Wording says "within the cited limit", never "safe"?

### 11.3 Not carried over

- The "FSIS freezes safe indefinitely" wording is kept only as the safety-versus-quality distinction; no thawing times, because no report gave them.
- Mercury species lists beyond those quoted: they differ across authorities and need checking against the user's country.
- Ready-reckoner shelf lives for opened dairy, sauces and tofu: no report offered a defensible figure, and the plugin avoids inventing one.
- Waste-reduction statistics and framing advice: held in `cooking-from-what-is-on-hand.md`.
- Nutrient, iron, fibre and protein advice for the same life stages: held in the nutrition reference, not here.
- Coverage: seven on-hand reports (Claude, ChatGPT, Consensus, Copilot, DeepSeek, Gemini, Perplexity) and five nutrition reports (Claude, Consensus, Copilot, DeepSeek, Perplexity). Consensus and ChatGPT are shallow; Copilot's hazard coverage is brief. Gaps: FSANZ, Health Canada, Japan, eggs and sprouts outside pregnancy, immunocompromised-specific rules, thawing.
