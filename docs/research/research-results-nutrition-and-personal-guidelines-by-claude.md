# Evidence Base for an AI Cooking Assistant: Dietary Patterns, Cooking Levers, Life Stages, Allergens, Nutrient Data and Safety Boundaries (November 2026)

The assistant can safely cover healthier everyday cooking. It should rest on a single convergent evidence base: plant-forward patterns rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and unsaturated oils, low in processed meat, salt and added sugar. On top of that sit a small number of graded cooking levers, such as swapping saturated for polyunsaturated fat, viscous fibre, potassium-enriched salt substitutes and pairing vitamin C with plant iron. The assistant should treat allergens, life-stage food-safety rules and medical red flags as hard constraints that it never relaxes.

## TL;DR

- **The guidelines largely agree; one does not.** Mediterranean, DASH, Portfolio, Nordic, the EAT-Lancet Planetary Health Diet and most national guides (DGE 2024, Eatwell, Canada's Food Guide, NNR2023) point the same way. The US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030 (released 7 January 2026) are the notable outlier. They raise protein to 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, favour full-fat dairy and animal protein, and still keep saturated fat ≤10% of energy. The American College of Cardiology warned on 27 January 2026 that if the recommended animal protein foods and "healthy fats" such as butter and beef tallow are eaten, "saturated fat intake will exceed 10% of daily calories." The assistant should default to the consensus and flag this conflict.
- **The best-supported cooking levers are clear.** For LDL: replace saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat (WHO: strong recommendation), use oat/barley β-glucan (3 g/day, EU-authorised claim), plant sterols, nuts and soy, which combined in the Portfolio diet lower LDL by about 17% (−0.73 mmol/L) in a GRADE meta-analysis of 7 trials with 439 participants (Chiavaroli et al., 2018). For blood pressure: the DASH pattern plus less sodium, with potassium-enriched salt where appropriate (WHO 2025: conditional, adults only, excluding kidney disease). For fibre: at least 25–29 g/day (Reynolds et al., 2019). For iron: heme sources, or plant iron paired with vitamin C and kept apart from tea, coffee and calcium. "Raising HDL" through diet is not a valid goal.
- **Some things are never negotiable.** Regulated allergens (EU 14, US 9), infant rules (no honey before 12 months, no added salt, no whole nuts under 5), pregnancy food-safety rules (listeria and mercury), and medical red flags (CKD, insulin or sulfonylurea use, anticoagulants, eating disorders, anaphylaxis, pregnancy complications) are hard constraints or referral triggers. They are not optimisation targets. Nutrient estimates for home recipes carry substantial uncertainty and must be shown as approximate.

## Key Findings

1. **The patterns converge.** Every evidence-based pattern reviewed emphasises the same food groups. They differ mainly in emphasis: sodium (DASH), LDL-specific foods (Portfolio), local foods (Nordic) and environmental limits (EAT-Lancet, DGE, NNR). Their evidence strength differs more than their content.
2. **Evidence strength varies by pattern.** The DASH effect on blood pressure and the Portfolio effect on LDL rest on randomised controlled feeding trials and meta-analyses, so certainty is moderate to high for those surrogate outcomes. Evidence for the Mediterranean diet on hard cardiovascular endpoints is supportive but graded low to moderate by Cochrane (Rees et al., 2019).\[1\] The EAT-Lancet and Nordic evidence on disease outcomes is largely observational.
3. **US guidance changed in 2026.** The 10th edition of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans keeps the limits of saturated fat ≤10% of energy and sodium <2,300 mg.\[2\]\[3\] It newly prioritises protein "at every meal" at 1.2–1.6 g/kg, full-fat dairy and red meat.\[3\]\[4\] A 2026 ACC Scientific Statement (JACC) says the higher protein targets and full-fat animal-food emphasis "are not fully aligned with current evidence supporting replacement of saturated fats with unsaturated fats, particularly from plant sources." An international assistant should not use the US 2025–2030 protein target as a default.
4. **Germany's DGE (March 2024)** moved to a mathematical optimisation model covering health, environment and usual intake.\[5\] The diet is more than ¾ plant-based. Dairy is cut from 3 to 2 portions a day, meat and sausage are capped at a maximum of 300 g a week, and fish stays at 1–2 portions a week.\[6\] **NNR2023** caps red meat at 350 g a week and says processed meat should be "as low as possible".\[7\]
5. **Free nutrient data have improved.** USDA FoodData Central (CC0) has been joined since 16 December 2025 by the German BLS 4.0, now free under CC BY 4.0 with 7,140 foods and 138 nutrients.\[8\]\[9\]\[10\] Together with UK CoFID (2021, Open Government Licence) and ANSES-Ciqual 2025 (3,484 foods, Etalab Licence Ouverte 2.0), this gives open data for every major market.\[11\]\[12\]

## 1. Evidence-Based Dietary Patterns

| Pattern | Core principles | Evidenced outcomes | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mediterranean** | Abundant vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts; extra-virgin olive oil as main fat; fish; moderate dairy; little red/processed meat | CVD events (PREDIMED: HR 0.69 with EVOO and 0.72 with nuts vs. a low-fat control); modest improvements in lipids and BP\[13\]\[14\] | Cochrane 2019 (30 RCTs): "still some uncertainty". Evidence for risk-factor benefits in primary prevention is low to moderate. Secondary-prevention evidence is sparse.\[1\]\[13\] |
| **DASH** | Vegetables, fruit, low-fat dairy, whole grains, legumes and nuts; limits sweets, red meat and saturated fat; high in potassium, calcium, magnesium and fibre; lower-sodium variants | Blood pressure (meta-analyses: SBP −3.2 to −6.7 mmHg; larger with sodium restriction and in feeding trials); metabolic syndrome components\[15\]\[16\]\[17\]\[18\] | Moderate to high for BP. A 2026 meta-analysis rated the SBP and DBP effects "moderate" on GRADE.\[16\] |
| **Portfolio** | A low-saturated-fat base plus four "portfolio" components: viscous fibre (oats, barley, psyllium, legumes), plant sterols, nuts, and soy/plant protein | LDL −17% (−27% when combined with an NCEP Step II diet as intended), plus non-HDL-C, ApoB, CRP and BP; estimated 10-year CHD risk −13%\[19\]\[20\] | Chiavaroli et al. (2018): GRADE-assessed meta-analysis of 7 trials (439 participants), commissioned for the EASD guidelines.\[21\]\[22\] Moderate to high for LDL. CVD-event data are only from cohorts. |
| **Nordic (NNR2023)** | Mostly plant-based: vegetables, fruit, berries, pulses, potatoes, whole grains; ample fish and nuts; moderate low-fat dairy; red meat ≤350 g/week; processed meat minimal\[7\]\[23\] | Lower risk of CVD, T2D and colorectal cancer, largely via the food-group evidence | Built on systematic reviews and scoping reviews of RCTs and cohorts. Mostly observational for hard outcomes. Some recommendations (more legumes and potatoes, less poultry) are mainly environmental.\[24\] |
| **EAT-Lancet Planetary Health Diet (2019; updated October 2025)** | Plant-rich, minimally processed reference diet of about 2,400 kcal; flexible across cultures; small amounts of animal foods\[25\] | The 2025 Commission links adherence with lower T2D, CVD, certain cancers and obesity, and reports that adherence is linked to "a 27% lower risk of premature death, equivalent to preventing around 15 million premature deaths per year" | Modelling plus cohort data. Contested: critics question the very low red-meat reference (around 15 g/day) and the reliance on GBD TMREL estimates.\[26\] The 2019 report was criticised for not covering nutrient needs in pregnancy and of women well; the 2025 report refers to WHO supplement advice.\[27\] |
| **US DGA 2025–2030** | "Real food"; protein at every meal (1.2–1.6 g/kg); full-fat dairy (3 servings); vegetables, fruit, whole grains; limit highly processed foods and refined carbohydrates; saturated fat ≤10% of energy; sodium <2,300 mg\[3\]\[28\] | Stated aim: lower chronic disease\[29\] | Conflicts with its own advisory committee (DGAC 2025) on protein and fat. ACC: the recommended animal foods and "healthy fats" such as butter and tallow would push saturated fat above 10%.\[4\] |
| **UK Eatwell Guide** | Plate model: about ⅓ fruit and vegetables (≥5 a day), about ⅓ starchy wholegrain carbohydrates, beans/pulses/fish/eggs/meat, some dairy or alternatives, small amounts of unsaturated oils; 30 g fibre; ≤6 g salt | Population chronic disease prevention | Based on SACN evidence reviews. Recommendations about patterns and fibre are well supported. |
| **German DGE (2024)** | Nutrition circle (replaces the pyramid); >¾ plant foods; 5 portions of fruit and vegetables a day; 1 portion of legumes a week (125 g) and 25 g nuts a day; 10 g plant oil a day; ≥⅓ of grains as whole grain; 2 dairy portions; ≤300 g meat a week; 1 egg a week\[6\]\[30\]\[31\]\[32\] | Health and environment, from an optimisation model\[5\] | Transparent modelling built on systematic evidence. Quantities are for healthy adults aged 18–65 on about 2,000 kcal.\[31\] |
| **Canada's Food Guide (2019)** | Plate: ½ vegetables and fruit, ¼ whole grains, ¼ protein foods, with plant-based proteins chosen more often; water as the drink of choice; cook more often | Population chronic disease prevention | Health Canada evidence review. Moved away from serving counts. |

**What they share:** (1) vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and nuts as the foundation; (2) unsaturated plant oils (and fish) as the main fats instead of butter, lard or tropical oils; (3) limited red meat and minimal processed meat; (4) low salt, added sugar and ultra-processed foods; (5) water as the default drink; (6) cultural adaptability. In practice, the core "healthy pattern" logic can be written once and then localised with national portion sizes and food examples.

## 2. Cooking-Relevant Levers for Common Health Goals

### Blood lipids (LDL lowering)

| Lever | What the cook does | Expected effect and certainty |
|---|---|---|
| Swap saturated fat for polyunsaturated fat | Cook with sunflower, soybean, corn or rapeseed oil instead of butter, ghee, lard, coconut or palm oil; use soft margarine; take the skin off poultry; choose lean cuts | WHO (2023): keep SFA ≤10% of energy (*strong*). Replace with PUFA (*strong*), plant MUFA (*conditional*) or fibre-rich carbohydrates (*conditional*).\[33\] The WHO calls the LDL evidence "high certainty", with PUFA giving the biggest LDL fall, then MUFA, then carbohydrate.\[33\]\[34\] Swapping SFA for refined starch or sugar gives little benefit.\[35\] |
| Remove trans fat | Avoid partially hydrogenated fats | WHO: ≤1% of energy (*strong*)\[33\] |
| Soluble/viscous fibre | Oats and barley (3 g β-glucan/day ≈ 75–90 g oats), psyllium, legumes, aubergine, okra | EU-authorised claim (Reg. 1160/2011): "Oat beta-glucan has been shown to lower/reduce blood cholesterol", effect at 3 g/day.\[36\]\[37\] Portfolio component. Moderate to high certainty. |
| Plant sterols/stanols | Fortified spreads, yogurts or milks (1.5–3 g/day) | Commission Regulation (EU) No 686/2014: 1.5–2.4 g/day lowers blood cholesterol by 7–10%, and 2.5–3 g/day by 10–12.5%. Not suitable for children under 5 or in pregnancy or breastfeeding (EU labelling). High certainty for LDL; no outcome trials. |
| Nuts | About 30 g/day (DGE: 25 g) | Portfolio and PREDIMED component. Moderate. |
| Soy and legumes | Tofu, tempeh, edamame, soy milk, lentils and beans in place of meat | Portfolio component. Heart UK states that "each of the four cholesterol-lowering foods is capable of achieving a 5-10% reduction in LDL cholesterol." Moderate. |
| Dietary cholesterol (eggs, shellfish) | Moderate amounts | Smaller effect than SFA. The DGE allows 1 egg a week as part of a pattern, which reflects modelling rather than a cholesterol limit.\[6\] Low-to-moderate certainty for any specific limit. |

**HDL:** The assistant should not offer "raise HDL" as a goal. Mendelian randomisation studies and failed HDL-raising drug trials (niacin, CETP inhibitors) show that HDL-C is not causally protective.\[38\]\[39\]\[40\] Lifestyle factors such as losing excess weight, physical activity and not smoking change HDL, but cooking should target LDL/non-HDL, triglycerides and the overall pattern. A user who registers "raise HDL" should get a gentle reframe and a suggestion to discuss lipid targets with their clinician.

### Fibre

Reynolds et al. (Lancet, 2019; 185 cohorts, 58 trials) found the greatest risk reductions at 25–29 g of fibre a day.\[41\]\[42\] Dose-response data suggest that intakes above 30 g give more benefit.\[41\] Each extra 8 g a day was associated with 5–27% lower all-cause mortality, CHD, T2D and colorectal cancer.\[43\] According to the Lancet's release on Reynolds et al., most people worldwide eat less than 20 g a day, only 9% of UK adults reach 30 g, and US adults average 15 g. Cooking levers: legumes in soups and sauces, whole-grain swaps (DGE: at least ⅓ whole grain; Eatwell: wholegrain where possible), skin-on vegetables, nuts and seeds, oats. Increase fibre gradually and with fluids to limit gastrointestinal discomfort. This is especially relevant for older adults and people with IBS, where low-FODMAP needs are an individual matter for a dietitian.

### Iron

- **Heme iron** (meat, fish, poultry) is absorbed 2–3 times better than non-heme iron, and dietary inhibitors barely affect it. **Non-heme iron** (legumes, grains, leafy greens, fortified cereals) makes up more than 90% of dietary iron in most diets.\[44\]\[45\]
- **Enhancers:** vitamin C (citrus, peppers, tomato, berries) eaten in the *same meal*, and meat/fish/poultry ("meat factor"). EFSA has authorised the claim "vitamin C contributes to increasing non-heme iron absorption".\[46\] A systematic review of whole diets (Collings et al., 2013) confirmed the enhancer effects; the size of inhibitor effects across whole diets is less clear than in single meals.\[44\]
- **Inhibitors:** polyphenols in tea and coffee (serve between meals, not with them), calcium (the only inhibitor that affects heme iron too, so avoid pairing iron-rich meals with large milk or cheese portions), phytates (whole grains and legumes; soaking, sprouting, fermenting and sourdough reduce them), and soy, milk and egg proteins.\[46\]\[47\]\[48\]
- **Cooking:** For an iron-focused toddler meal, pair lentils or beef with tomato or pepper, serve water rather than milk or tea with it, and use fortified infant cereals.

### Blood pressure, sodium and potassium

- WHO: sodium <2 g/day, about 5 g salt (*strong*). US DGA: <2,300 mg.\[2\]\[49\] UK: ≤6 g salt.
- **DASH** reduces SBP by about 3–7 mmHg.\[15\]\[17\] A 2023 RCT (Filippou et al.) found that both DASH and Mediterranean diets on top of salt restriction lowered BP, with the Mediterranean diet better for office SBP.\[50\]
- **Potassium-enriched salt substitutes:** WHO (January 2025) "suggests replacing regular table salt with lower-sodium salt substitutes that contain potassium (*conditional* recommendation)". This applies to adults only and "not pregnant women or children". It excludes "individuals with kidney impairments or with other circumstances or conditions that might compromise potassium excretion", such as people on potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements.\[49\]\[51\] **The assistant must screen for this before suggesting a substitute.**
- Cooking levers: cut salt gradually (taste adapts); use herbs, spices, acid (lemon, vinegar) and umami; limit stock cubes, soy sauce, cured meats and cheese; rinse canned beans; choose potassium-rich foods (beans, leafy greens, potatoes, bananas).

### Blood glucose and type 2 diabetes

- Levers: carbohydrate *quality* (whole grains, legumes, intact grains; fibre ≥25–29 g), fewer sugary drinks and refined starches, Mediterranean, DASH or Portfolio patterns, portion consistency, eating vegetables and protein first, cooling and reheating starches (resistant starch has a modest, low-certainty effect), and the EU-authorised claim that oat/barley β-glucan reduces post-meal glucose rises. EFSA adopted a new opinion on oat β-glucans and post-meal glucose peaks in January 2026; authorisation was still pending at the time of reporting.\[52\]\[53\]
- People with diagnosed diabetes **on insulin or sulfonylureas** risk hypoglycaemia if carbohydrate is changed sharply. Low-carbohydrate meal plans for these users require clinician involvement (see Section 7).

### Weight management

Energy balance is what matters. No macronutrient ratio consistently beats others over the long term. Cooking levers with practical support: high-fibre, high-volume vegetables; adequate protein; fewer energy-dense ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks; measuring oil (1 tbsp ≈ 120 kcal); plate models; consistent portions. Certainty for any specific culinary trick is low. The assistant should not set calorie targets for children, pregnant users or anyone flagged for disordered eating.

### Gut health

The evidence supports fibre diversity (legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts) and fermented foods as part of a healthy pattern. Claims about specific "microbiome-optimising" foods or probiotic cures are low certainty or unsupported (Section 8). People with IBS, IBD or coeliac disease need individual dietetic care.

### Cooking methods

- **Vitamin losses:** USDA Retention Factors Release 6 (2007), covering 16 vitamins, 8 minerals and alcohol for about 290 foods, shows that greens boiled in water and drained keep about 55% of vitamin C and 60% of folate. Steamed vegetables keep about 85% of both, and stir-fried greens about 85%. Legumes boiled for 2–2.5 hours keep only 30% of folate. Minerals mostly stay at 85–100%.\[54\]\[55\] **Levers:** steam, microwave or stir-fry; use little water and reuse it in soups; don't overcook; cut vegetables just before cooking.
- **Oil for heating:** Choose oils high in unsaturated fat that are stable for the method. Rapeseed/canola, olive (including extra-virgin) and high-oleic sunflower suit everyday sautéing and roasting. Keep oils rich in omega-3 or polyunsaturated fat (flaxseed, walnut) for cold use. Avoid reusing frying oil repeatedly. Avoid charring and very high-heat grilling of meat. Lower-temperature methods and marinades reduce heterocyclic amines; a moderate-certainty mechanistic precaution.
- **Alcohol does not "cook off"** completely: USDA factors show 75% retained when flamed and still 5% after 2.5 hours of simmering.\[55\] This matters for pregnancy, children, people in recovery from alcohol dependence and religious preferences.

## 3. Life-Stage Requirements

### Infants and toddlers

- **Iron:** Stores run low at around 6 months. Offer iron-rich foods (meat, fish, legumes, fortified cereals, egg) from the start of complementary feeding, paired with vitamin C. Avoid large amounts of cow's milk in toddlers, as it displaces iron-rich food and inhibits absorption.
- **Salt:** NHS: "Do not add any salt to your baby's food or cooking water"; babies should have <1 g salt a day. Avoid stock cubes, gravy, bacon and crisps.\[56\]\[57\] **Family cooking rule:** season after setting the baby's portion aside.
- **Added sugar:** none in the first year (NHS). US guidance says no added sugars before age 2. Advice for later childhood varies by country (some UK guidance to age 4), so it should be configurable by country.\[58\]
- **Honey:** none before 12 months because of infant botulism risk (NHS, HSE, AAP).\[56\]\[59\]\[60\]\[61\] This applies to honey cooked into foods as well.
- **Choking:** NHS says no whole nuts under 5 (use smooth nut butter or ground nuts; peanut butter on its own is also a choking hazard). Halve grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthways, flatten blueberries, avoid popcorn, hard sweets and raw hard carrot pieces.\[58\]\[61\]\[62\] Always supervise, seated upright.
- **Allergens:** Current UK and US guidance supports introducing common allergens (egg, peanut as butter or ground) from around 6 months, one at a time.\[62\]\[63\] Infants with severe eczema or existing allergy need clinician advice before peanut introduction.
- **Textures:** smooth or mashed at around 6 months, progressing to lumpy and finger foods by 7–9 months and family foods by 12 months.\[59\]
- **Other:** NHS lists rice drinks, raw shellfish, unpasteurised cheese and shark, swordfish or marlin as unsuitable.\[59\] Cow's milk can be the main drink from 12 months.\[58\]\[60\]\[62\]

### Children

Same healthy pattern, smaller portions. Limit sugary drinks and free sugars. Do not apply adult restrictive diets such as very-low-fat, plant sterols, calorie deficits or the US 1.2–1.6 g/kg protein target without professional input. FDA/EPA: children should eat fish from the "Best Choices" (lowest-mercury) list in age-appropriate portions.\[64\]

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

- **Listeria:** avoid unpasteurised milk and cheese; mould-ripened and soft blue cheeses unless cooked until steaming hot; pâté; refrigerated smoked fish unless cooked. Reheat ready-meals until steaming.
- **Raw/undercooked foods:** avoid raw or undercooked meat and raw shellfish. Cook fish thoroughly (FDA/EPA advise against raw fish such as sushi in pregnancy).\[65\]\[66\] **Countries differ on eggs:** the UK allows runny British Lion-stamped eggs, while US guidance calls for eggs cooked until firm. The assistant must use the user's country rule.
- **Mercury:** FDA/EPA (2021 advice) recommends 2–3 servings a week (8–12 oz) from "Best Choices" or 1 a week from "Good Choices". Avoid king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, Gulf of Mexico tilefish and bigeye tuna.\[64\]\[67\]\[68\] The UK (NHS) says avoid shark, swordfish and marlin and limit oily fish and tuna; exact limits are country-specific.
- **Vitamin A:** UK guidance advises avoiding liver and liver products in pregnancy.
- **Alcohol:** none (including "cooked-off" wine, see above). **Caffeine:** about 200 mg a day maximum (NHS/EFSA).
- **Salt substitutes:** WHO excludes pregnant women from its potassium-salt recommendation.\[49\]
- Pregnancy complications (gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, hyperemesis) require clinical care (Section 7).

### Older adults

- **Protein:** PROT-AGE and ESPEN recommend ≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy people over 65, and 1.2–1.5 g/kg for people with acute or chronic illness. The exception is severe CKD (eGFR <30, not on dialysis), where protein may need to be *limited*.\[69\]\[70\]\[71\] Aim for about 25–30 g of protein per meal.\[72\]\[73\]\[74\] These are expert-consensus positions with moderate-certainty evidence. Note the contrast with the general adult RDA of 0.8 g/kg and the UK RNI of 0.75 g/kg.\[2\]\[75\]\[76\]
- Other cooking considerations: soft or easy-to-chew textures for dentition or dysphagia (dysphagia itself needs a speech-language therapist and IDDSI-level textures, so refer); vitamin D and B12 (supplementation is a clinician decision); taste changes (use herbs and acid rather than salt); smaller, energy-dense meals when appetite is poor; food-safety vulnerability.

## 4. Allergies, Intolerances and Preferences

**Regulated major allergens**

| EU (Regulation 1169/2011, Annex II): 14 | US (FALCPA + FASTER Act): 9 |
|---|---|
| Cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide/sulphites (>10 mg/kg), lupin, molluscs\[77\] | Milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame (added by the FASTER Act 2021, effective 1 January 2023)\[77\]\[78\]\[79\] |

Other jurisdictions such as Canada and Australia/NZ have their own lists.\[80\] The assistant should handle the *union* of lists plus anything the user names. Note the US "bakery loophole": some manufacturers *added* sesame to products to avoid cross-contact labelling duties. The FDA called this "not consistent with the spirit of the FASTER Act" but has not prohibited it.\[81\]\[82\]

**Cross-contact basics for home cooks:** cook the allergen-free meal first; use clean pans, utensils and boards; wash hands; never use shared frying oil; read every label every time, as recipes change;\[79\] treat "may contain" warnings as a "no" for anyone with severe allergy; watch for hidden sources (e.g. sesame in tahini or "spices", milk in "whey" or "casein", egg in glazes, fish in Worcestershire sauce, celery in stock, mustard in dressings, lupin in some gluten-free flours).

**Definitions**

- **Allergy:** an immune-mediated reaction, often IgE-mediated, that can be life-threatening (anaphylaxis) even at trace exposure. **Coeliac disease** is immune-mediated (not IgE) and also requires strict, trace-level avoidance of gluten.
- **Intolerance:** non-immune; usually dose-dependent and not life-threatening (e.g. lactose intolerance, where small amounts may be tolerated).
- **Preference:** ethical, religious, cultural or taste-based (vegetarian, halal, kosher, dislikes).

**Hard constraints the assistant must never relax:** (1) declared allergies and coeliac disease, with trace-level avoidance and cross-contact warnings; (2) life-stage safety rules such as honey under 12 months, choking hazards and pregnancy listeria/mercury rules; (3) clinician-prescribed restrictions such as potassium or phosphorus limits in CKD, vitamin K consistency on warfarin, or grapefruit with interacting drugs; (4) religious or ethical exclusions declared as absolute, such as halal, kosher or vegan. The last group is not a safety issue, but violating it breaks trust. Intolerances should be treated as **dose-based constraints** at the threshold the user states. Preferences are **soft** and may be suggested only with explicit opt-in, never sneaked in. An LLM should never "substitute" an allergen with a related food, such as one tree nut for another or crustacean for mollusc, without the user confirming tolerance.

## 5. From Goals to Practical Cooking Rules

**How dietitians translate guidelines:**

- **Food lists (prefer / limit / avoid):** e.g. for LDL, *prefer* oats, legumes, nuts, rapeseed or olive oil, fish; *limit* butter, cheese, fatty processed meat, coconut oil; *avoid* (only where clinically required) trans fats.
- **Swap lists:** butter → rapeseed oil or soft spread; cream → yogurt plus a little cornflour; white rice → brown rice, barley or bulgur; half the minced meat → lentils; salt → lemon, herbs, garlic.
- **Plate models:** Eatwell (about ⅓ fruit and vegetables, about ⅓ starchy wholegrains), Canada (½ vegetables and fruit, ¼ whole grains, ¼ protein), Harvard and diabetes plate methods.
- **Portion guides:** hand-based measures, or national portion grams (e.g. DGE: legumes 125 g cooked, nuts 25 g, oil 10 g).\[31\]

**A recommended structure for a personal guideline document (per household member):**

1. **Profile:** age band and life stage (infant, toddler, child, pregnant, breastfeeding, adult, older adult), country (which sets the safety-rule set), and approximate size if needed.
2. **Hard constraints (never relaxed):** allergens with severity and cross-contact sensitivity; coeliac disease; clinician-prescribed limits, recorded only as the user reports them, not invented; medication-related food rules; absolute religious or ethical exclusions.
3. **Safety rules triggered automatically by the profile:** e.g. toddler: no added salt, no honey before 12 months, choking-safe cuts; pregnancy: listeria, mercury, eggs per country, no alcohol.
4. **Targets (soft, quantified, evidence-tagged):** e.g. "fibre ≥30 g/day", "SFA <10% of energy", "include iron + vitamin C source at lunch and dinner". Each target is tagged with its evidence source and certainty level.
5. **Preferences:** cuisines, dislikes, time and budget, equipment, skill.
6. **Review triggers:** conditions under which the assistant must stop and refer (Section 7).

**Cooking one meal for several people: example of an adult with high LDL and a toddler who needs iron.** Use a "base + branch" method. Cook a shared, unsalted base such as a lentil-and-beef ragù made with rapeseed oil, tomatoes and peppers (heme plus non-heme iron, with vitamin C from the tomato and pepper). Use lean beef or half beef and half lentils to keep SFA down. Before seasoning, set aside the toddler's portion, soft and mashed or cut to a safe size. For the adult, add salt to taste or a potassium-based salt if eligible, serve over barley or whole-grain pasta for β-glucan and fibre, and add a side salad with walnuts. Serve water to both (no tea with the toddler's meal, and avoid a large glass of milk at the same time). This plan meets the adult's LDL levers and the toddler's iron and salt rules from a single pot. The general principle: **the strictest safety rule applies to the shared base, and individual targets are met through branches, sides and toppings.**

**Accuracy limits of estimating a home-cooked recipe:**

1. **Natural variability:** composition varies with cultivar, season, soil, ripeness, breed and feed, fat trim and fortification practice (which differs by country). Database values are averages.\[87\]
2. **Matching error:** mapping "1 onion" or "a splash of oil" to a database entry and a gram weight is often the largest error source.
3. **Cooking changes:** use *yield factors* (water loss or gain, fat uptake or drip) *and* *retention factors*. USDA's True Retention formula is %TR = (Nc × Gc)/(Nr × Gr) × 100.\[55\] Without this, vitamin C or folate in boiled vegetables can be overestimated by 40% or more.
4. **Absorbed oil and discarded liquids:** frying fat uptake and drained cooking water are hard to estimate.
5. **Portioning:** dividing a pot "into 4" is rarely equal.
6. **Bioavailability:** e.g. non-heme iron absorption depends on the meal context, not the iron content.\[90\]

**Recommendation:** show nutrient values as rounded ranges (e.g. "about 8–10 g fibre per serving"), state the database used, apply retention and yield factors where available, and do not present estimates as precise enough for clinical dosing such as insulin carbohydrate counting or renal potassium limits without a disclaimer.

## 7. Boundaries and Red Flags

**Where general healthy eating stops and medical nutrition therapy (MNT) starts.** The assistant supports healthy eating patterns. MNT, meaning individual assessment, diagnosis-specific prescriptions and monitoring, is the role of registered dietitians and physicians. Situations that need MNT or clinician input:

| Condition / situation | Why general advice can harm | Assistant behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic kidney disease** | Potassium, phosphorus, sodium, protein and fluid needs are individual. Standard "healthy" advice (potassium salt, more legumes, high protein for older adults) may be dangerous. | Never suggest a potassium salt substitute (WHO excludes kidney impairment).\[49\] Follow only clinician-set limits entered by the user; otherwise refer. |
| **Diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas** | Carbohydrate changes can cause hypoglycaemia | General recipes are fine. Do not prescribe carbohydrate targets or low-carb plans; advise discussing changes with the care team. |
| **Anticoagulants (warfarin/VKAs)** | Swings in vitamin K intake (leafy greens) change INR | Advise *consistent* vitamin K intake rather than avoidance; refer for specifics. DOACs have no comparable vitamin K interaction. |
| **Grapefruit/Seville orange/pomelo** | CYP3A4 inhibition raises levels of some statins (e.g. simvastatin), calcium-channel blockers, some immunosuppressants and others | If the user lists medications, flag that they should check with a pharmacist; do not judge the interaction on its own. |
| **Potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs** | Risk of high potassium with potassium salts | No potassium salt substitutes without clinician approval.\[51\] |
| **MAOIs** | Tyramine (aged cheese, cured meats, fermented soy) | Refer. |
| **Eating disorders or disordered eating** | Calorie counting, "good/bad food" lists and weight targets can worsen illness | No calorie or weight targets. Neutral language. Signpost to specialist services if there are signs such as severe restriction, compensatory behaviours, rapid weight loss or distress around food. |
| **Severe allergy/anaphylaxis history, multiple food allergies in children** | Risk of nutritional inadequacy and fatal exposure | Hard constraints; suggest an allergist or dietitian for growth and nutrition planning. |
| **Pregnancy complications** (gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, hyperemesis), and **infants** with faltering growth or refusal of solids | Need individual monitoring | Refer. |
| **Unexplained weight loss, dysphagia, persistent GI symptoms, blood in stool, signs of anaemia, malnutrition in older adults** | May signal disease | Refer to a doctor promptly. |
| **Very-low-calorie, ketogenic or extended fasting regimes; supplements at high doses** | Medical risks | Not designed by the assistant; refer. |

**Regulatory and ethical considerations.**

- **EU:** Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 allows only authorised nutrition and health claims (e.g. the oat β-glucan claim with its conditions of use). Disease-risk-reduction claims (Art. 14) need specific authorisation.\[36\]\[91\] Recipe marketing such as "this recipe lowers cholesterol" may count as a health claim in commercial communication. Use descriptive language instead, e.g. "contains oats, a source of β-glucan; 3 g/day as part of a varied diet helps lower cholesterol".
- **US:** FDA-authorised health claims, qualified health claims and structure/function claims each have different standards. Disease-treatment claims are not allowed for foods.
- **Software regulation:** software intended to diagnose or treat disease may fall under medical-device rules (EU MDR; FDA device and general-wellness policies). Keeping the assistant in the "general wellness / healthy eating" space, with no diagnosis, no individual therapeutic prescriptions and no medication-dosing support, is the main mitigation. This needs legal review per market.
- **Data protection:** health and allergy data are special-category data under GDPR. Explicit consent, minimisation and security are required.
- **Ethics:** be transparent about uncertainty; avoid weight stigma; be culturally inclusive; do not give commercial supplement recommendations.

**An appropriate disclaimer covers:** (1) general information to support healthy eating, not medical advice, diagnosis or treatment; (2) not a replacement for a doctor, registered dietitian or pharmacist, especially for listed conditions, medications, pregnancy and infants; (3) users are responsible for verifying ingredient labels for allergens, and cross-contact cannot be guaranteed; (4) nutrient values are estimates; (5) seek emergency help for allergic reactions; (6) guidance follows a named national authority based on the user's country. Disclaimers supplement, and do not replace, the in-conversation referral behaviour above.

## 8. Popular Claims to Avoid

| Claim | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| "Superfoods" (goji, açaí, kale, spirulina cure or prevent disease) | Marketing term. Benefits come from overall patterns; no single food has proven disease-preventing powers. The EU bans "superfood"-type health claims unless they are authorised. |
| "Detox" diets, juices, cleanses | The liver and kidneys detoxify. No credible evidence that detox products remove "toxins"; some are harmful. |
| "Coconut oil is heart-healthy" | High in SFA; raises LDL. WHO advises replacing SFA with PUFA.\[33\] |
| "Butter or tallow are healthy fats" (popularised partly by US DGA 2025–2030 messaging) | Conflicts with WHO, AHA, ACC, DGAC and every European guideline; SFA raises LDL (high certainty).\[4\]\[33\] |
| "Seed oils are toxic or inflammatory" | Replacing SFA with PUFA from seed oils lowers LDL and is linked to lower CHD (WHO: strong recommendation).\[33\]\[35\] Repeated deep-frying is a separate issue. |
| "Raise your HDL with food X" | HDL-C is not causal; target LDL.\[92\] |
| "Alkaline diets/water change blood pH" | Blood pH is tightly regulated; no clinical basis. |
| "Apple cider vinegar/cinnamon/lemon water melts fat or cures diabetes" | At most small, low-certainty effects; not a treatment. |
| "Eggs are banned / eggs are unlimited" | Both overstated. Eggs fit in moderate amounts; SFA has the bigger effect on LDL. |
| "Gluten-free is healthier" | Only for coeliac disease or diagnosed sensitivity; GF products are often lower in fibre. |
| "Carbs make you fat / one macro ratio is best for weight" | Energy balance and diet quality matter; carbohydrate *quality* (fibre) predicts outcomes. |
| "Everyone needs 1.2–1.6 g/kg protein" (US DGA 2025–2030) | The RDA for healthy adults remains 0.8 g/kg.\[2\]\[93\] Higher intakes have support in older adults (PROT-AGE) and athletes.\[69\]\[74\] The US target is contested and should not be applied to children or people with CKD. |
| "Microwaving destroys nutrients" | Microwaving with little water often retains water-soluble vitamins *better* than boiling. |
| "Alcohol cooks off completely" | 5–75% remains depending on method (USDA).\[55\] |
| "Supplements (antioxidants, multivitamins) prevent heart disease or cancer" | RCTs largely null for the general population; some high-dose supplements are harmful (e.g. beta-carotene in smokers). Targeted supplements (folic acid before conception, vitamin D, B12 for vegans, iron where deficient) are clinician- or authority-guided. |
| "Raw honey is safe for babies / natural sugars are fine" | Honey is unsafe under 12 months; honey, syrups and juices count as free sugars.\[58\]\[61\] |
| "Red meat is essential for iron" / "Plant diets can't meet iron needs" | Heme iron is better absorbed, but well-planned plant meals with vitamin C pairing meet needs for most people. Higher-risk groups (infants, menstruating and pregnant people) need attention, not alarm. |

## Recommendations

1. **Use a consensus "healthy pattern" core** (Section 1 commonalities), localised by country (DGE, Eatwell, Canada, NNR, US). Where the US DGA 2025–2030 diverges (protein, full-fat dairy, animal fats), use WHO, DGAC and AHA positions for cardiovascular goals and state the disagreement openly.
2. **Tag every lever with its certainty level** (e.g. "WHO strong", "EFSA-authorised", "meta-analysis moderate", "low/observational") in the system knowledge base, and expose a plain-language version to users.
3. **Implement a three-tier constraint engine:** hard constraints → automatic life-stage and country safety rules → soft targets → preferences. Allergens and safety rules should be checked deterministically, not left to LLM judgement alone.
4. **Base + branch cooking** for multi-member households, with the strictest safety rule applied to the shared base.
5. **Use open nutrient databases** (FDC CC0, BLS 4.0 CC BY, CoFID OGL, Ciqual Etalab) with retention and yield factors. Show values as rounded estimates with their source.
6. **Have hard-coded referral triggers** (Section 7) and a guideline-drafting flow that asks screening questions first: medications, kidney disease, pregnancy, eating-disorder history, allergies. The flow should decline to draft therapeutic guidelines for those conditions.
7. **Build a claims filter** that blocks unauthorised health claims and the myths in Section 8.

## Caveats

- Some details here rest on secondary summaries rather than the primary documents: the US DGA 2025–2030 specifics (e.g. "no added sugars, at most 10 g per meal", dropping numeric alcohol limits) and parts of the EAT-Lancet 2025 quantities. Verify them against the primary texts before encoding them as rules.
- Mediterranean diet evidence for hard endpoints remains contested (Cochrane low/moderate certainty; PREDIMED was retracted and re-published with re-analysis in 2018).
- The 15-million-deaths EAT-Lancet figure is a model projection, not an observed outcome.\[94\]
- Infant added-sugar age cut-offs, egg safety in pregnancy and fish limits differ by country. Use per-country rule sets.
- The EFSA opinion on oat β-glucans and post-meal glucose (January 2026) was not yet an authorised claim at the time of reporting.\[52\]\[53\] Check the EU Register before using the wording.
- Regulatory classification of AI nutrition tools (medical device vs. wellness) is evolving and market-specific; obtain legal advice.
